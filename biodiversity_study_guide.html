Living and non-living things

Tuesday, September 1 · Learning goal: identify the characteristics of living and non-living things
Life processes are the basic, continuous jobs that living organisms carry out to maintain their bodies, stay alive, and prevent breakdown.

If something carries out those jobs, it is living. If it never has and never will, it is non-living. Everything in your environment falls into one group or the other.

Living things

  • Alive
  • Breathe and use oxygen
  • Move on their own
  • Blood flows, heart circulates (in animals)
  • Eat, or make their own food
  • Need nutrients to grow

Non-living things

  • Never alive
  • No heartbeat, no breathing
  • Idle — only moved by something else
  • Can't eat or take in nutrients
  • Can't sleep, grow or reproduce
  • But living things still depend on them

Watch out — the trap in your notes

Your notebook lists "deceased" and "mummified" under non-living. Careful: scientists split this into three groups. Living (a mango tree), dead — once living (a dried leaf, a fossil, a wooden desk), and non-living — never alive (a rock, seawater, sunlight). A dead thing was never non-living; it was living first. Examiners love this one.

Biotic and abiotic factors

Wednesday, September 2 · Living and non-living things in our environment
Biotic factors are the living parts of an ecosystem.
Abiotic factors are the non-living parts of an ecosystem.
Factor simply means something that affects a situation.
Ecosystem — a community of living things together with the non-living surroundings they interact with.

Key science fact: living things (biotic factors) carry out essential life processes such as nutrition, respiration, growth and reproduction. Non-living things (abiotic factors) do not carry out these processes — but living things depend on them to survive.

The wonder of biodiversity

Monday, September 8 · Discovering why Earth needs every animal to survive
Biodiversity — the variety of living organisms in an ecosystem. Widened out, it is the immense variety of all life on Earth: every living organism from tiny bacteria and plants to animals and humans, along with the communities and ecosystems they come from.
Ecosystem — living organisms interacting with their physical environment.
Habitat — the place where an organism lives, finds food and gets shelter.
Resilience — the ability of an ecosystem to recover after a disruption and return to a healthy state.

Key point: greater variety means a stronger, healthier ecosystem for all living things.

Three words people mix up

A habitat is an address — where one organism lives. An ecosystem is the whole neighbourhood — every organism plus the soil, water, light and air they interact with. Biodiversity is the head count of different kinds living in it.

How ecosystems stay balanced

Tuesday, September 9 · Four jobs, done by different species

Across the world, species depend on one another. Four jobs keep the whole system turning — tap each to see who does it.

Plants

Tap
Make food using sunlight. They are the starting point of almost every food chain, so everything above them depends on them.

Pollinators

Tap
Bees, birds, bats and butterflies help plants reproduce by carrying pollen from flower to flower. No pollinators, no seeds or fruit.

Predators

Tap
Keep prey numbers balanced. Without them one species would multiply and eat out the whole food supply.

Decomposers

Tap
Fungi, worms and bacteria break down dead matter and return nutrients to the soil so plants can use them again.

Why it holds together: biodiversity creates many connections. If one species struggles, another can often step in and keep the system working.

Think it through: the mangrove decision

Somewhere in the world, people plan to clear a coastal mangrove to build shops. What ecological risks should they consider before deciding?

Show a strong answer

Tap to open
  • Mangrove roots are a nursery habitat — young fish shelter there, so fish catches fall for years afterwards.
  • The roots trap sediment and break wave energy, so the coast loses its natural storm and erosion defence in hurricane season.
  • Biodiversity drops — birds, crabs and fish lose food, shelter and breeding places at once.
  • Less biodiversity means lower resilience: after the next storm the coast recovers more slowly, or not at all.
  • Nutrients that mangroves normally cycle into the water are lost, affecting nearby seagrass and coral reefs too.

Disruption and resilience

When something big goes wrong — and how nature comes back
Disruption — a sudden or major change that harms an ecosystem.
Resilience — the ability of an ecosystem to recover after a disruption and return to a healthy state.

A disruption can remove food, shelter or breeding places. The effects reach coral reefs, grasslands, forests, lakes, wetlands and even city parks around the world. Biodiversity supports resilience: if one species is affected, another may help keep the ecosystem working.

How biodiversity fights disease

Thursday, September 10 · The dilution effect
The dilution effect (ecosystem level) — when an ecosystem has high species diversity, pathogens such as viruses, bacteria and parasites have a harder time spreading.
Host regulation — many pathogens rely on one specific competent host species to replicate in. In a diverse ecosystem, a disease-carrying insect such as a tick or mosquito is far more likely to land on an incompetent host — a species the pathogen cannot replicate in.
Buffered transmission — every landing on an incompetent host "wastes" the pathogen's chance to spread. That dilutes the disease's prevalence in the environment and protects vulnerable species.

The question you got wrong — fix it now

Why does a forest with many different tree species recover faster after disease strikes?

✗ "Animals will instantly plant new seeds to replace fallen trees." Animals do disperse seeds, but not instantly and not on purpose — this isn't the reason.

✓ A disease usually attacks one species. In a mixed forest the other tree species survive, so the canopy, the soil and animal shelter are all maintained while the affected species regrows. In a single-species forest, one disease can take everything down at once.
